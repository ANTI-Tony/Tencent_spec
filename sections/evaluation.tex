\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{One frozen checkpoint.} Every experiment in this section runs on one checkpoint that we froze before the first run: the controller, trainer, and harness code, the six patched engine files, the two calibration files, the factory drafters, the target weights, the prompt pools, the seeds, and the software environment, each recorded by hash (\cref{app:manifest}). The simulator of \cref{sec:heldout} reads the same frozen calibration file. \pol{TIDE} was added later on the same checkpoint as new files only and, like the reruns below, runs in an environment rebuilt from the frozen package list.

\paragraph{Workload.} Each run serves three phases on one H100 GPU: 200 chat requests at concurrency 16 (phase A1), then 16{,}000 unique requests from a new domain at concurrency 256, which saturate the GPU for \nv{bdur.min}--\nv{bdur.max}\,s (phase B), then 200 different chat requests (phase A2). The three scenarios are Qwen3-8B with code in B, Qwen3-8B with Chinese in B, and Qwen3-30B-A3B with code in B. Decoding is greedy, outputs stop naturally or at 512 tokens, and the B prompts were never seen during calibration. All policies except \pol{Production} reserve the trainer's memory (\cref{app:manifest}).

\paragraph{Policies.} \pol{Production} runs the factory drafter with speculation always on, without export or memory reserve, as deployed today. \pol{Static} does the same under the evaluation's engine configuration and is the reference for all differences. \pol{Plain} serves without a drafter. \pol{Switch} applies \cref{eq:switch} with the domain's frontier and never trains. \pol{Threshold} turns speculation off whenever the server is saturated and trains at duty 0.5 whenever TPOT is within the SLO, i.e., the idle-time rule of OSD \citep{liu2024osd} combined with a load threshold. \pol{TIDE} reimplements the training triggers of TIDE \citep{park2026tide} with the speculation rule of \pol{Switch}: it trains at duty 0.5 from the start and whenever acceptance drifts down, swaps in a drafter every 200 requests learned, and restores the previous one and stops if acceptance did not rise. \pol{Always} trains at duty 0.5 from the first request and swaps drafters after 100, 200, 400, \dots{} requests. \pol{Informed} trains at duty 0.5 from the moment phase B starts until it has learned from 800 requests, swapping at 100, 200, 400, and 800; we chose its stopping point from the learning curves of \cref{fig:motivation}c, so it is a reference that knows when to stop rather than a deployable policy. \pol{Full} is the controller of \cref{sec:method}. \pol{Always} and \pol{Informed} never turn speculation off (\cref{app:policies}).

\paragraph{Repetitions and metrics.} Every policy runs three times per scenario. Repetition $r$ fixes the prompt order of all phases and the trainer's random seed, so within a repetition all policies serve the same request sequence, and we report paired differences as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over the three pairs. The main metric is phase-B output throughput; we also report TPOT and whole-run throughput. The \nv{n.runs} runs used \nv{hosts} nodes of the same type, and the whole evaluation took \nv{gpuh} GPU-hours; the coefficient of variation of B throughput was at most \nv{cv.static.max}\% for the static policies. A scan with a rule fixed in advance found node interference in \nv{scan.flagged.word} of the \nv{scan.n} runs, all of them baselines; we report their reruns, which can only narrow the controller's margins (\cref{app:rerun}).

Table~\ref{tab:behaviour} shows that the controller takes qualitatively different actions in the three scenarios.

\paragraph{Chinese: turn speculation off.} The structural-ceiling test decides \nv{zh.full.why.structural_ceiling}\% of the ticks: even the best reachable drafter (\nv{lc.zh.n3200}) is below break-even at this load, so the controller keeps speculation off except for short probes and spends almost no time training (mean duty \nv{zh.full.duty}). Relative to \pol{Plain} it ends at \nv{zh.full_vs_plain}\%, the cost of the engine's speculation-off state (\pol{Switch}: \nv{zh.switch_vs_plain}\%; \cref{sec:overheads}). \pol{Informed}, which cannot turn speculation off, spends its training on a drafter that can never pay; \pol{Full} is \nv{zh.full_vs_informed}\% relative to it.

\paragraph{Qwen3-8B code: adapt and keep speculating.} The controller never turns speculation off. It enters phase B with acceptance \nv{code.full.acc0} instead of the factory \nv{code.static.acc0}, because it already trained in the SLO slack of phase A1 and the chat-trained drafter transfers to code; during B it trains at a mean duty of \nv{code.full.duty}, swaps \nv{code.full.swaps} drafters, and reaches \nv{code.full.acc1}. The value rule of \cref{eq:value} declines to train in \nv{code.full.why.saturated_no_value}\% of the phase-B ticks. The adapted drafter makes speculation as fast as plain decoding at $C{=}256$ (\nv{code.full_vs_plain}\%). This is \nv{code.full_vs_switch}$\pm$\nv{code.full_vs_switch.sd}\% relative to \pol{Switch}, about as much as the engine's speculation-off state costs (\pol{Switch} is \nv{code.switch_vs_plain}\% relative to \pol{Plain}); \pol{Plain}, which has no such overhead, is as fast as \pol{Full} here. For a dense target at saturation, adaptation thus mainly lets speculation escape the regime where the factory drafter loses.

\paragraph{MoE code: adaptation is the whole gain.} Turning speculation off would halve throughput, and the controller keeps it on. It trains at a mean duty of \nv{moe.full.duty}, swaps \nv{moe.full.swaps} drafters, raises acceptance from \nv{moe.full.acc0} to \nv{moe.full.acc1}, and then stops at the ceiling (\nv{moe.full.why.at_ceiling}\% of ticks). Its \nv{moe.full.d}\% over \pol{Static} comes entirely from adaptation: relative to \pol{Switch} it is \nv{moe.full_vs_switch}$\pm$\nv{moe.full_vs_switch.sd}\%, while \pol{Threshold}, whose load rule turns speculation off, loses \nv{moe.thresh.abs}\%.

\paragraph{Against the informed schedule.} Relative to \pol{Informed}, \pol{Full} is \nv{code.full_vs_informed}\% on Qwen3-8B code, within the run-to-run spread (\nv{code.full_vs_informed.min} to \nv{code.full_vs_informed.max}\% per repetition), and \nv{moe.full_vs_informed}\% on MoE, with the same sign in all three repetitions (\nv{moe.full_vs_informed.min} to \nv{moe.full_vs_informed.max}\%). \pol{Always}, which never stops training, ends close to \pol{Static} (\nv{code.always.d}\%, \nv{zh.always.d}\%, \nv{moe.always.d}\%): continuous training costs as much throughput as the better drafter gains. We therefore do not claim that the controller beats a well-chosen fixed schedule where one exists; it matches such schedules within 1.5\% without knowing when to stop, and it does not fail where they do.

\paragraph{Against acceptance-triggered training.} \pol{TIDE} speculates by the same frontier but trains when observed acceptance suggests it (\cref{tab:tide}). On Chinese it trains for \nv{zh.tide.trainB}\% of phase B: acceptance is measured only while speculation is on, so its long average still reflects chat traffic, and a drift trigger restarts training within two seconds of \nv{zh.tide.rejdrift} of its \nv{zh.tide.rej} rejections. None of its drafters reaches break-even, and \pol{Full} is \nv{zh.full_vs_tide}\% relative to it. On code, \pol{TIDE} stops after the first round that does not raise acceptance: beyond 400 requests, a round of 200 requests adds at most \nv{tide.roundgain.max} on the calibration curves, within the $\pm$\nv{tide.noise.m2} spread of its 8\,s comparison, whereas doubling export gaps keep later gains measurable. \pol{Full} is \nv{code.full_vs_tide}\% (\nv{code.full_vs_tide.min} to \nv{code.full_vs_tide.max}\% per repetition; \nv{code.full_vs_tide.env}\% when also run in \pol{TIDE}'s rebuilt environment) and \nv{moe.full_vs_tide}\% (\nv{moe.full_vs_tide.min} to \nv{moe.full_vs_tide.max}\%) relative to it. With 4$\times$ larger rounds and a 3$\times$ larger drift margin, \pol{TIDE} trains less on Chinese (\nv{zh.tv.trainB}\% of B), yet \pol{Full} is still \nv{zh.full_vs_tv}\% relative to it (\nv{zh.full_vs_tv.min} to \nv{zh.full_vs_tv.max}\%), and \nv{code.full_vs_tv}\% and \nv{moe.full_vs_tv}\% on code (\cref{app:tidevar}). Acceptance-triggered training thus comes close where adaptation pays and fails where the drafter cannot pay.

\subsection{Held-out validation in a frozen simulator}\label{sec:heldout}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig5_heldout.pdf}
\caption{Frozen-simulator validation on unseen workloads. (a) Goodput of the controller relative to the best of five simple policies in each of \nv{v1.n} trace windows (sorted). (b) Median goodput relative to \pol{SpecAlways} for bursts of a new domain, by burst length; labels give the share of strict wins. (c) Largest surge, in multiples of plain-decoding capacity, served without SLO violation in 20 scenarios (dots) and their mean (bars).}
\label{fig:heldout}
\end{figure*}

The live runs cover one workload shape. To test other traffic, we replay traces in a simulator built from the same frozen calibration file: a fluid single-GPU model in which arrivals set the operating concurrency, excess demand queues, training slows serving by $\kappa(C)\,d$, and acceptance follows the measured learning curves. The simulator implements the controller's rules, with goodput (tokens served within the SLO) instead of throughput as the currency under saturation, and five simple policies: \pol{NoSpec}, \pol{SpecAlways}, and three that keep speculation on and train at full duty when a trigger fires: \pol{OSD} (fewer than 16 running requests), \pol{Timed} (30\,s every 5 minutes), and \pol{AccDrop} (acceptance below 95\% of the ceiling, an acceptance-triggered rule in the spirit of TIDE). We froze the simulator and the calibration (\cref{tab:manifest}) and declared four criteria before running workloads never used in development: \nv{v1.n} unseen trace windows from BurstGPT \citep{wang2024burstgpt} and the Azure 2023 traces \citep{patel2024splitwise} at three or four load scales; 60 random bursts of a new domain; 40 Chinese regimes; and 20 surge scenarios at seven surge levels (\cref{app:sim}).

Figure~\ref{fig:heldout} and \cref{tab:heldout} in \cref{app:sim} report the outcomes. The goodput criterion holds in all \nv{v1.n} trace windows: the controller is never more than \nv{v1.short}\% below the best simple policy and is the best policy in \nv{v1.best}. The training criterion fails in four Azure chat windows, where \pol{OSD} trains on fewer tokens than the controller (\cref{app:sim}). Three expectations did not survive. First, training pays back reliably only for long bursts: short bursts are mostly ties, strict wins reach \nv{v2.d60.win}\% at 60\,s and \nv{v2.d180.win}\% at 180\,s. Second, the controller ranks above \pol{SpecAlways}, \pol{OSD}, and \pol{AccDrop} in all \nv{v3.n} Chinese regimes, but its advantage over \pol{SpecAlways} is \nv{v3.feas.min}--\nv{v3.feas.max}$\times$ at feasible saturation and as low as \nv{v3.over.min}$\times$ under overload, so the pre-declared $1.5\times$ margin fails. Third, \pol{AccDrop} tolerates surges as well as the controller (mean \nv{v4.acc} versus \nv{v4.cm}); the controller's advantage lies in meeting all four criteria at once.

\subsection{Latency and overheads}\label{sec:overheads}\label{sec:latency}

Faster serving at saturation also shortens tail latency: \pol{Full} changes phase-B p99 TPOT by \nv{code.full.p99}\%, \nv{zh.full.p99}\%, and \nv{moe.full.p99}\% relative to \pol{Static}. Training in the SLO slack has a price at light load: on Qwen3-8B the median TPOT of phase A1 rises by \nv{code.full.a1}\% and \nv{zh.full.a1}\%, still far below the \nv{slo.m1}\,ms SLO; a latency-oriented deployment would cap the duty of the slack rule (\cref{app:latency}). The export path and the trainer's memory reserve cost \nv{moe.infra}--\nv{code.infra}\% of phase-B throughput (\pol{Production} versus \pol{Static}). The engine's speculation-off state is \nv{zh.switch_vs_plain.abs}--\nv{code.switch_vs_plain.abs}\% slower than serving without a drafter. Part of it is the first seconds of B, served with speculation on; the rest, the cost of a loaded but idle drafter, is the largest overhead. Drafter swaps (\nv{swap.m1.min}--\nv{swap.m1.max}\,s for Qwen3-8B, \nv{swap.m2.min}--\nv{swap.m2.max}\,s for MoE) and speculation switches (at most \nv{switch.max}\,ms) did not cause a single failed request in the \nv{n.runs} runs (\cref{app:extra}).
