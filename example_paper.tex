\begin{abstract}
Online adaptation can improve a speculative-decoding drafter, but higher token acceptance does not necessarily translate into faster serving. The profitability of speculation depends on workload and target architecture, while training on the serving GPU competes directly with inference. We examine these trade-offs using EAGLE-3 drafters for Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B, measuring load-dependent speculation gains, training interference, and domain-specific learning limits. Based on these measurements, we develop a workload-aware controller that decides when to speculate, how much to train, and when to stop. It combines a calibrated profitability frontier with drafter learning curves and training opportunity costs, using available serving slack for adaptation and limiting training under saturation to cases where the expected gains justify its cost. In a frozen vLLM evaluation of 81 paired runs across three workloads and nine policies, the controller improves saturated throughput over static speculation by 18.0\%, 87.7\%, and 8.0\%, while staying within 4.7\% of the best measured policy in every scenario. Ablations show that the gains arise from different decisions: disabling unprofitable speculation on Chinese traffic, limiting training interference on dense code, and adapting the drafter on the MoE target. Mixed-traffic experiments reveal a near-break-even case where training fails to recover its cost. The controller makes the same decisions on SGLang, although speculation-off overhead leaves plain decoding faster on Chinese. In a frozen simulator, it stays within 0.6\% of the best simple policy across 35 unseen trace windows, while reliable training payback requires bursts lasting roughly three minutes or longer. The goal is not to keep improving the drafter, but to improve serving performance when adaptation pays for itself.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Speculative decoding lets a small drafter propose several tokens that the target model verifies in a single forward pass \citep{leviathan2023fast,chen2023accelerating}. Its benefit depends on how many proposals the target accepts, and a drafter trained offline loses acceptance when the traffic differs from its training data. Several systems therefore keep adapting the drafter while it serves, using the target's own outputs as supervision \citep{liu2024osd,qian2026drafts,wang2026aurora,park2026tide}. On our code workload, a factory EAGLE-3 drafter for Qwen3-8B raises its acceptance length from 2.12 to 2.72 after learning online from 3{,}200 requests.

A better drafter does not by itself make serving faster. Figure~\ref{fig:motivation} shows three effects that a serving system has to weigh (\cref{sec:measure}). First, whether speculation pays at all depends on load and architecture: for Qwen3-8B with short outputs, speculation is 59\% faster than plain decoding with one request in flight but 14\% slower with 256, whereas on the Qwen3-30B-A3B mixture-of-experts (MoE) target it is still 50\% faster with 256. Second, training on the serving GPU is not free: a trainer running a fraction $d$ of the time removes roughly $d/2$ of the serving throughput. Third, drafters reach very different ceilings: the Chinese drafter stalls at acceptance 1.45, far below the 2.28 it needs to beat plain decoding at saturation.

These effects couple the two decisions a serving system has to make, whether to use the drafter and whether to improve it. Training helps only if the improved drafter will be used, which depends on the load; and at saturation, using the drafter may become worthwhile only after training. Sharing the GPU is attractive because online adaptation needs little compute (the controller trained on at most about 0.8 million tokens per run, so a dedicated training GPU, as in Aurora and TIDE, would mostly sit idle), but every second of wasted training, e.g., on a drafter that can never reach break-even, is lost serving.

We build a controller for this co-located setting (\cref{sec:method}). Every second it decides whether to speculate, how much of the GPU the trainer may use, and when the trainer should export a new drafter or stop. It relies on a calibration made once per model and domain before deployment: a profitability frontier, the break-even acceptance length as a function of concurrency, and a learning curve. Speculation is enabled only above break-even; training uses the latency slack below the service-level objective (SLO) when there is slack, runs under saturation only if the gain predicted over a forecast horizon repays the throughput it costs, and stops when the reachable acceptance cannot cross break-even or no longer rises. We implement the controller in vLLM \citep{kwon2023vllm} with live feature export, drafter hot replacement, and a runtime speculation switch (\cref{sec:system}), and port it to SGLang (\cref{sec:sglang}).

We evaluate with every component frozen in one checkpoint (\cref{sec:setup,sec:evaluation}): 81 paired runs over three scenarios (Qwen3-8B code, Qwen3-8B Chinese, Qwen3-30B-A3B code), nine policies, and three repetitions. The best fixed policy differs by scenario: plain decoding for Chinese, and a training schedule that is told when to stop for the two code scenarios. Every other policy falls at least 8.8\% short of the per-scenario best in some scenario; the controller stays within 4.7\% in all three. 
We further evaluate the controller in a frozen simulator using unseen workload traces and randomized drift scenarios, with pre-declared criteria for goodput, training efficiency, and workload robustness (\cref{sec:heldout}).

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A measurement study of how speculation, co-located training, and online adaptation interact on a dense and an MoE target, including where a better drafter cannot pay off (\cref{sec:measure}).
\item A controller that couples a calibrated profitability frontier with learning curves to decide when to speculate, how much to train, and when to stop, implemented in vLLM and ported to SGLang (\cref{sec:method,sec:system,sec:sglang}).
\item A frozen-checkpoint evaluation with paired repetitions against nine policies, including an informed schedule and a TIDE-style policy, with ablations, mixed traffic under stale or missing labels, a second engine, and a frozen-simulator validation on unseen traces with pre-declared criteria, reported together with the claims it weakens (\cref{sec:evaluation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Runtime control of speculative decoding.}
Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive generation by verifying draft tokens in parallel while preserving the target model's output distribution \citep{leviathan2023fast,chen2023accelerating,miao2024specinfer}. Draft architectures such as Medusa and EAGLE-3 improve the efficiency of token proposals \citep{cai2024medusa,li2024eagle,li2024eagle2,li2025eagle3}. Beyond draft quality, serving performance depends on when and how speculation is used. TurboSpec \citep{liu2024turbospec} selects a speculation length, including zero, based on estimated goodput; AdaSpec \citep{huang2025adaspec} accounts for latency SLOs; Nightjar \citep{li2025nightjar} disables unprofitable speculation and offloads the drafter; and FASER \citep{chen2026faser} adjusts speculation length per request. MoESD \citep{huang2025moesd} and MagicDec \citep{sadhukhan2025magicdec} further show that the profitability of speculation depends on target architecture, batch size, and sequence length. These methods optimize speculative execution given a drafter. Our setting adds a second decision: whether spending resources to improve that drafter will increase future serving throughput enough to justify the cost.

\paragraph{Online drafter adaptation.}
Online speculative decoding addresses draft--target mismatch by updating the drafter from serving traffic. OSD \citep{liu2024osd} uses online distillation to adapt to changing query distributions, while OnlineSPEC \citep{qian2026drafts} develops online-learning algorithms with dynamic-regret guarantees. DVI \citep{bhansali2025dvi} learns from acceptance and rejection signals, and related work explores online drafter selection \citep{liu2026notabandit} and co-training during reinforcement-learning rollouts \citep{wang2026cotraining}. These approaches primarily improve how a drafter learns from available feedback. Our controller treats the learning process itself as a serving decision: higher acceptance is useful only when the resulting throughput gain justifies the resources consumed by adaptation.

\paragraph{Joint training and serving: Aurora and TIDE.}
Aurora \citep{wang2026aurora} closes the training--serving loop through asynchronous reinforcement learning from live inference traces, using a separate training server and hot-swapping updated drafters into serving. Its emphasis is on rapid adaptation, feedback efficiency, and deployment without extensive offline draft training. TIDE \citep{park2026tide} goes further in runtime coordination: it reuses target-model hidden states for training, enables speculation according to batch-dependent profitability, and selectively resumes training when acceptance drifts, retaining updates that improve acceptance. Its deployment separates inference and training across GPU resources, including heterogeneous accelerators. Both systems demonstrate that drafter training and speculative serving can operate together, and TIDE already controls when to speculate and when to adapt. Our work addresses a different resource constraint. Training shares the serving GPU, so each update directly reduces the capacity available to process requests. We therefore estimate not only whether speculation is profitable at the current load, but also whether further adaptation can improve serving enough to repay its interference cost over the expected workload duration. Per-domain learning estimates help identify cases where improvement is insufficient, while the payback calculation limits training even when the drafter can continue to improve.

\paragraph{Co-located training and inference.}
Resource sharing between training and inference has also been studied outside speculative decoding. FlexLLM \citep{oliaro2024flexllm} co-schedules inference with fine-tuning workloads under latency constraints. Our setting differs because training changes the model used by the serving process itself: allocating more GPU time to training immediately reduces serving capacity but may improve subsequent speculative decoding. The controller makes this trade-off explicitly, allocating training from available slack under light load and evaluating its expected net benefit under saturation.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig2.pdf}
\captionsetup{skip=2pt}
\caption{The control loop. Each second the controller combines engine and trainer measurements with the frozen calibration and applies three tests: whether the current drafter beats plain decoding (\cref{eq:switch}), whether training can still pay (structural ceiling, ceiling, convergence), and how much to train (SLO slack, or the value of \cref{eq:value} under saturation). Its outputs are a speculation switch, the trainer's duty cycle, and drafter exports that are hot-swapped into the running engine.}
\label{fig:controller}
\end{figure*}
\paragraph{Setting and objective.} One GPU serves a target model with an EAGLE-3 drafter and also runs a trainer that updates a copy of the drafter from features exported during serving (\cref{sec:system}). The drafter proposes $k{=}3$ tokens per step and the target verifies them in one forward pass, so the acceptance length $a$, the number of tokens emitted per verification step, lies between 1 and 4. Every second the controller reads the running requests $C$, the queue, the smoothed acceptance length $\hat a$, and the trainer's progress. An operator-provided label $\ell$ names the current traffic domain, for example a product line; a \emph{regime} is a period with one label. The controller sets whether speculation is enabled ($z\in\{0,1\}$), the trainer's duty cycle $d\in\mathcal D=\{0,0.1,0.25,0.5,1\}$, and when the trainer exports a drafter, which is then hot-swapped into the server. Its objective is the output throughput served over the regime under an SLO that bounds the time per output token (TPOT) by twice the single-request decode time, $\Tslo$ (16.7\,ms for Qwen3-8B, 10.6\,ms for the MoE target); it pursues this objective one second at a time, from the predictions below. Figure~\ref{fig:controller} summarizes the loop.

\paragraph{Profitability frontier.} At concurrency $C$, speculation pays when its throughput exceeds that of plain decoding, i.e., when the ratio $G(a,C)$ of the two exceeds one. Verification makes every step more expensive, and at high concurrency there is no spare compute to absorb this, so the break-even acceptance $a^{*}(C)$ at which $G=1$ rises with load. The ratio is close to linear in acceptance at every load (\cref{sec:measure}), so for each domain $\ell$ we fit, from the throughput of a few drafter checkpoints at a few loads,
\begin{equation}
\Ghat_\ell(a,C)=\alpha_\ell(C)\,a+\beta_\ell(C),\qquad a^{*}_\ell(C)=\frac{1-\beta_\ell(C)}{\alpha_\ell(C)}.\label{eq:frontier}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Calibration.} Before deployment we measure, once per target, the plain and speculative step times and plain throughput as functions of $C$, the interference coefficient $\kappa(C)$ (serving throughput falls by $\kappa(C)\,d$ at duty $d$), and the trainer's token rate $r$ at full duty; and once per domain, the frontier of \cref{eq:frontier} and the learning curve $a_\ell(N)$, the acceptance after learning from $N$ requests (\cref{sec:measure,app:calibration}). From these the controller predicts the throughput $\Theta(a,z,C)$ and the TPOT for any acceptance, speculation state, and duty (\cref{app:controller}). It never trains on a domain without a learning curve.

\paragraph{When to speculate.} Speculation is enabled when the current drafter beats plain decoding at the current load,
\begin{equation}
z=\mathbf{1}\big[\Ghat_\ell(\hat a,C)>1\big]\wedge\big(\widehat T_{\mathrm{spec}}\le\Tslo\vee\widehat T_{\mathrm{plain}}>\Tslo\big),\label{eq:switch}
\end{equation}
where the second clause keeps plain decoding if speculation would push an otherwise compliant load out of the SLO. A switch needs three agreeing ticks and happens at most once per 10\,s; since acceptance cannot be measured with speculation off, every drafter swap is followed by an 8\,s probe with speculation on.

\paragraph{Whether training can pay at all.} Let $\amax_\ell=\max_N a_\ell(N)$ be the domain's ceiling. If even the ceiling loses to plain decoding at the current load, $\Ghat_\ell(\amax_\ell,C)\le1$, no amount of training makes speculation worthwhile: the controller does not train, and \cref{eq:switch} turns speculation off instead (the \emph{structural-ceiling} test). Training also stops when the drafter is within 0.02 of its ceiling, or when the last swapped drafter raised the measured acceptance by less than 0.02.

\paragraph{How much to train.} If the predicted TPOT is within the SLO and no request is queued, the GPU has spare capacity and training costs latency but no throughput. The controller then picks the largest $d\in\mathcal D$ whose predicted TPOT stays below $0.9\,\Tslo$, i.e., $T_0/(1-\kappa(C)\,d)\le0.9\,\Tslo$, where $T_0$ is the predicted TPOT without training. Under saturation (requests queued, or $C$ at 95\% of the batch limit), training costs throughput immediately. The controller sets a target acceptance $a^{\dagger}=\hat a+0.8\,(\amax_\ell-\hat a)$, reads from the learning curve how many training tokens $n$ separate $\hat a$ from $a^{\dagger}$, and scores each $d\le0.5$ by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V(d)={}&\big[\Theta^{\star}(a^{\dagger})-\Theta(\hat a)\big]\,\max\!\big(0,\widehat H-\tau(d)\big)\\
&-\Theta(\hat a)\,\kappa(C)\,b(d)\,\tau(d).
\end{aligned}\label{eq:value}
\end{equation}
Here $\tau(d)=n/\min(rd,\rho)$ is the training time at data rate $\rho$, $b(d)=\min(d,\rho/r)$ the trainer's busy fraction, $\Theta^{\star}$ the better of speculation on and off, and $\widehat H$ the forecast remaining regime length (its age so far, clipped to $[90,600]$\,s): the first term is the gain once the improved drafter is in place, the second the loss while it trains. The controller trains at $\arg\max_d V(d)$ if the maximum is positive and not at all otherwise.

\paragraph{Export, swap, and assumptions.} The trainer exports a drafter after 100, 200, 400, \dots{} requests learned in the current regime, each swapped in immediately; a label change flushes the trainer's buffer and restarts the schedule. The controller needs the label and a per-domain calibration from a short profiling run, but neither the regime's end nor the right time to stop; its rules and constants (\cref{app:controller}) were fixed before the evaluation checkpoint was frozen and not changed afterwards.

\section{System and Experimental Setup}\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Implementation in vLLM}\label{sec:impl}

We implement the system on vLLM 0.27.1 (V1 engine) with three engine extensions and two processes beside the server.

\paragraph{Live feature export.} The trainer needs the target's auxiliary hidden states at every accepted position. Inside the EAGLE-3 path of the model runner we copy them to pinned host memory without blocking the step, and a background thread writes them per request into a shared-memory ring buffer; rejected positions are dropped, only tensor-parallel rank 0 writes, and a full buffer drops one step instead of stalling serving. Prefix caching has to be disabled, because cached positions have no hidden states.

\paragraph{Co-located trainer.} A separate process on the same GPU trains the drafter with cross-entropy against the target's own next tokens (AdamW; \cref{app:manifest}), reading completed requests from the ring buffer into a last-in-first-out buffer with a 60\,s age limit, and implements duty cycle $d$ by sleeping $(1-d)/d$ times the duration of each step.

\paragraph{Drafter hot swap and speculation switch.} A collective RPC loads new drafter weights into all workers without restarting the engine (median 0.71\,s for Qwen3-8B and 0.31\,s for MoE in the frozen runs). A second RPC switches speculation in the scheduler and the workers together, so drafting stops instead of being computed and discarded; the drafter stays in GPU memory and the round trip takes a median of 22\,ms.

\paragraph{Controller process.} A 1\,Hz process reads the engine's metrics endpoint and the trainer's log, writes the duty cycle to a file the trainer reads before every step, and requests exports through a command file.

\subsection{Experimental protocol}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{One frozen checkpoint.} Every experiment runs on one checkpoint that we froze before the first run: the controller, trainer, and harness code, the six patched engine files, the two calibration files, the factory drafters, the target weights, the prompt pools, the seeds, and the software environment, each recorded by hash (\cref{app:manifest}). \pol{TIDE} was added later as new files only (\cref{app:manifest}).

\paragraph{Workload.} Each run serves three phases on one H100 GPU: 200 chat requests at concurrency 16 (phase A1), then 16{,}000 unique requests from a new domain at concurrency 256, which saturate the GPU for 258--600\,s (phase B), then 200 different chat requests (phase A2). The scenarios are Qwen3-8B with code or Chinese in B and Qwen3-30B-A3B with code in B. Decoding is greedy, outputs stop naturally or at 512 tokens, and the B prompts were never seen during calibration. All policies except \pol{Production} reserve the trainer's memory (\cref{app:manifest}).

\paragraph{Policies.} \pol{Production} runs the factory drafter with speculation always on, without export or memory reserve; \pol{Static} does the same under the evaluation's engine configuration and is the reference for all differences; \pol{Plain} serves without a drafter; \pol{Switch} applies \cref{eq:switch} with the domain's frontier and never trains; \pol{Threshold} turns speculation off whenever the server is saturated and trains at duty 0.5 whenever the predicted TPOT is within the SLO (OSD's idle-time rule \citep{liu2024osd} plus a load threshold); \pol{TIDE} reimplements TIDE's training triggers \citep{park2026tide} (drift-restarted rounds of 200 requests at duty 0.5, kept if acceptance rose) with the speculation rule of \pol{Switch}; \pol{Always} trains at duty 0.5 from the first request and swaps drafters after 100, 200, 400, \dots{} requests; \pol{Informed} trains at duty 0.5 from the start of phase B until it has learned from 800 requests, a stopping point read off \cref{fig:motivation}c (a reference that knows when to stop, not a deployable policy); \pol{Full} is the controller of \cref{sec:method} (\cref{app:policies}).

\paragraph{Repetitions and metrics.} Every policy runs three times per scenario; repetition $r$ fixes the prompt order and the trainer's seed, so all policies of a repetition serve the same request sequence, and we report paired differences as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over the three pairs. The main metric is phase-B output throughput; we also report TPOT. The 81 runs used 7 nodes of the same type and 27.4 GPU-hours; a scan with a rule fixed in advance found node interference in five of them, all baselines, and we report their reruns, which can only narrow the controller's margins (\cref{app:rerun}).

\paragraph{Further conditions.} Beyond the matrix we run four ablations of \pol{Full}, one test removed at a time (\cref{app:ablation}); two 50/50 mixtures of code or Chinese with chat, calibrated as domains of their own, with \pol{Full} also run under a stale label and without any label (\cref{app:mix}); five policies on a port to SGLang 0.5.10 with its own frozen calibration (\cref{app:sglang}); and a fluid simulator built from the frozen calibration, on unseen traces against four pre-declared criteria (\cref{app:sim}).

\section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Speculation profitability and training cost}\label{sec:measure}

The calibration measurements (\cref{app:calibration}; Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}) show the three effects that make speculation and training one decision.

\paragraph{Whether speculation pays depends on load and architecture.} For Qwen3-8B with short outputs, speculation is 59\% faster than plain decoding at $C{=}1$ but 14\% slower at $C{=}256$ (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}a); with long outputs it remains 71\% faster at $C{=}96$. The MoE target behaves the other way: its gain grows to 66\% at $C{=}64$ and is still 50\% at $C{=}256$, because a speculative step costs only 1.36$\times$ a plain step there, against 2.20$\times$ on the dense target \citep{huang2025moesd}.

\paragraph{Co-located training is not free.} At duty cycle $d$ the trainer computes for a fraction $d$ of the time, and serving throughput falls almost linearly, by $\kappa(C)\,d$ with $\kappa$ between 0.41 and 0.58 (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}b): duty 0.5 costs 22--29\% of throughput, and training all the time costs 50--61\%.

\paragraph{Learning ceilings differ, and the frontier is linear.} After learning online from $N$ requests (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}c), the code and SQL drafters improve steadily, the Chinese drafter stalls at 1.45 because only 42.6\% of the target's output tokens are in its 32k draft vocabulary, and the function-calling drafter peaks at $N{=}200$ and then declines. Against the break-even line at $C{=}256$, the code drafter crosses after about 100 requests, the Chinese drafter never does, and the MoE drafter is above its much lower break-even (1.27) from the start. The throughput ratio of three or four drafter checkpoints at six loads is close to linear in acceptance at every load (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} in \cref{app:calibration}): the fit of \cref{eq:frontier} has residuals of at most 0.031 (code), 0.013 (Chinese), and 0.017 (MoE), and the adapted code drafter reaches 1.17$\times$ plain decoding there against 0.93$\times$ for the factory drafter. Training is therefore worth doing only if the trained drafter will be used, which depends on load, and at saturation the drafter may become usable only after training.

\paragraph{Chinese: turn speculation off.} The controller acts differently in the three scenarios (\cref{tab:behaviour}). On Chinese the structural-ceiling test decides 97\% of the ticks: even the best reachable drafter (1.45) is below break-even at this load, so the controller keeps speculation off except for short probes and barely trains (mean duty 0.016). It ends $-4.7$\% from \pol{Plain}, the cost of the engine's speculation-off state (\pol{Switch}: $-3.1$\%; \cref{sec:overheads}).

\paragraph{Qwen3-8B code: adapt and keep speculating.} The controller never turns speculation off. It enters phase B with acceptance 2.50 instead of the factory 2.13, because it already trained in the SLO slack of phase A1 and the chat-trained drafter transfers to code; during B it trains at a mean duty of 0.067, swaps 3 drafters, reaches 2.66, and the value rule declines to train in 77\% of the ticks. The adapted drafter makes speculation as fast as plain decoding at $C{=}256$ ($-0.1$\%), which is $+4.6$$\pm$2.5\% relative to \pol{Switch}, about what the engine's speculation-off state costs.

\paragraph{MoE code: adaptation is the whole gain.} Turning speculation off would halve throughput, and the controller keeps it on; it trains at a mean duty of 0.084, swaps 4 drafters, raises acceptance from 2.68 to 2.97, and stops at the ceiling (43\% of ticks). Relative to \pol{Switch} it is $+8.3$$\pm$0.7\%, while \pol{Threshold}, which turns speculation off, loses 46.0\%.

\paragraph{Why acceptance-triggered training falls short.} \pol{TIDE} speculates by the same frontier but trains when observed acceptance suggests it (\cref{tab:tide} in \cref{app:extra}). On Chinese it trains for 70\% of phase B, because acceptance is measured only while speculation is on, so its drift trigger keeps restarting training (within two seconds of 7 of its 9 rejections) for drafters that never reach break-even; \pol{Full} is $+21.6$\% relative to it. On code it stops after the first round that does not raise acceptance: beyond 400 requests a round of 200 adds at most 0.03, within the $\pm$0.03 spread of its 8\,s comparison, whereas doubling export gaps keep later gains measurable; \pol{Full} is $+3.7$\% relative to it on Qwen3-8B code, positive in every repetition, and $+2.0$\% on MoE, within the spread, also with larger rounds and a larger drift margin (\cref{app:tidevar}).

\paragraph{Which test the gain comes from.} \Cref{tab:ablate} removes one test at a time (\cref{app:ablation}). The gain comes from a different test in each scenario. On Chinese it comes entirely from the switch: without it the controller is $-43.8$\% below \pol{Full} ($-46.2$ to $-42.0$\% per repetition), while the other three variants stay within noise. On Qwen3-8B code it comes from training, and the payback rule is worth 2.5\%: at a fixed duty the trainer runs until the ceiling test stops it (mean duty 0.16 against 0.067), and the interference costs more than the last drafters gain. On the MoE target every variant trains and stops at the ceiling. The stop tests add nothing under saturation ($-0.2$\%, $+1.8$\%, $-0.9$\%), because near the ceiling the payback rule already finds no value. The variant without labels or learning curves stays within noise of \pol{Full} in all three scenarios ($-0.6$\%, $-0.1$\%, $-0.9$\%): the switch needs only the measured acceptance and the per-target step times, and the measured swap gains replace the learning curve.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Improving drafter acceptance does not always improve serving throughput, and the value of adaptation differs across workloads. On Qwen3-8B Chinese, disabling speculation is more effective than further training; on the MoE target, speculation remains profitable and adaptation provides the main gain. Qwen3-8B code falls between these cases: training improves the drafter, but excessive training can consume more serving capacity than the improvement recovers. The ablations confirm that the payback rule limits this interference under saturation, while the explicit stopping tests contribute little beyond the payback decision in that setting. The code--chat mixture exposes a harder case. Although the calibrated learning ceiling exceeds the break-even acceptance, the cost of reaching it is not recovered over the observed workload. Training decisions near this boundary are therefore sensitive to calibration errors and the remaining duration of a workload. The controller also requires operator-provided domain labels and per-domain calibration, and does not initiate training without the required calibration. The fixed-mixture experiments cover stale and missing labels, but not continuously drifting workload compositions. Even with an accurate decision, the realized benefit depends on the serving engine: disabling speculation does not fully recover plain-decoding performance when the drafter remains loaded, as observed in both vLLM and SGLang (\cref{sec:sglang}). The controller also relies on predicted throughput and TPOT rather than directly enforcing measured latency (\cref{app:controller}). Our live evaluation is limited to two serving engines, H100 GPUs, two Qwen3 targets, and EAGLE-3 drafters with $k{=}3$, using greedy decoding without prefix caching. Workloads follow a synthetic chat--new-domain--chat sequence or one of two fixed mixtures; other traffic patterns are examined only in simulation. The TIDE-style baseline reproduces its training triggers on a shared GPU rather than its original deployment. Each configuration has three paired repetitions across 7 nodes, with interference-affected runs replaced under the documented protocol (\cref{app:rerun}). Small differences between adaptive policies remain within run-to-run variability, and reordering prompts from the development pools does not establish generalization to unseen prompts.

% ---- sections/conclusion ----

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied speculative decoding as a serving decision in which using and improving a drafter must be weighed against their costs under the current workload. Our measurements show that better acceptance does not necessarily translate into higher serving throughput: the profitability of speculation changes with concurrency and target architecture, while online adaptation consumes resources that would otherwise serve requests. To address this trade-off, we developed a workload-aware controller that combines a calibrated profitability frontier, drafter learning estimates, and training opportunity costs to decide when to speculate, how much to train, and when to stop. Across three workloads spanning dense and MoE targets, the controller stays within 4.7\% of the best measured policy in every scenario, without knowing the optimal training stopping point in advance. The ablations reveal that different workloads benefit from different decisions: disabling unprofitable speculation is crucial for Chinese traffic, controlling training interference matters for dense code, and continued adaptation benefits the MoE target. Mixed-traffic experiments further show that a drafter capable of crossing the profitability boundary may still cost more to train than it returns, while the SGLang deployment demonstrates that correct control decisions remain subject to engine-specific execution overheads. 
The goal is not to keep improving the drafter, but to improve serving performance when adaptation pays for itself.


\section*{Impact Statement}
This work aims to make the serving of large language models more efficient; serving the same traffic with fewer GPU-hours lowers the cost and energy use per request. We see no consequences specific to it beyond those of deploying language models in general.

\paragraph{Engine.} vLLM 0.27.1 (V1 engine), one H100 GPU per run, \texttt{max\_num\_seqs} 256, maximum model length 4{,}096, prefix caching disabled, greedy decoding, outputs of at most 512 tokens with natural stopping. GPU memory utilization is 0.90 for \pol{Production} and 0.90 minus the trainer's reserve of 18\,GB (Qwen3-8B) or 5\,GB (MoE) for all other policies (0.708 and 0.847); every policy except \pol{Production} that loads a drafter also runs the export path. EAGLE-3 drafters with $k{=}3$, auxiliary target layers $(2,18,33)$ for Qwen3-8B and $(2,24,45)$ for Qwen3-30B-A3B, and a 32k draft vocabulary. The feature export uses a 4\,GB shared-memory ring and samples every step.

\paragraph{Trainer.} Cross-entropy on the target's next tokens over the draft vocabulary; AdamW with learning rate $5\times10^{-5}$, betas $(0.9,0.95)$, no weight decay, 10 warm-up steps, and gradient clipping at 1.0; about 4{,}096 tokens per step and at most 2{,}048 tokens per sequence; a last-in-first-out buffer of at most 600k tokens with a 60\,s age limit; duty cycle by sleeping $(1-d)/d$ times the step time. The trainer's seed is the repetition index.

\paragraph{Environment rebuild.} After the first 72 runs, the cluster's scratch purge (files not accessed for 60 days) deleted part of the frozen software environment: Python sources whose compiled copies were cached had never been re-read. \subsection{Interference scan and reruns}\label{app:rerun}

One \pol{TIDE} run on the MoE target lost about a third of its phase-B throughput two minutes into the phase, while its trainer was idle and its acceptance length and concurrency stayed unchanged. To treat every run alike, we then fixed a rule and applied it to all 81 runs. A 5\,s sample of phase B is \emph{clean} if at least 90\% of the 256 request slots are busy, the trainer was idle for the preceding 10\,s, and neither a speculation switch nor a drafter swap happened in those 10\,s. Its reference is the median throughput of the 50 clean samples from other runs of the same scenario and speculation state whose acceptance length is closest. A run is flagged if the median ratio to the reference stays below 85\% over some 60\,s of consecutive clean samples.

The rule flagged five runs: the \pol{TIDE} run and four runs of \pol{Switch} and \pol{Threshold} in the original matrix, none of \pol{Full} (\cref{tab:rerun}). Every unflagged run stays at or above 0.88 of its reference. The 9 runs of \pol{Always} cannot be scored, because their trainer is never idle for 60\,s. In the two flagged \pol{Switch} runs the drop starts right after speculation is switched off, but the switch itself does not explain it: over the 25 switches from speculation on to off in phase B, the median throughput of the first minute after the switch is 1.00 of that of the second minute, and only these two runs fall below 0.9. We reran each flagged run with its unchanged configuration on a different node; none of the reruns is flagged, and the main results use the reruns ($\dagger$ in \cref{tab:runs}). Because only baselines were flagged, the replacement can only narrow the controller's margins. With the original runs instead, the best policy of each scenario and the worst-case shortfalls of \pol{Full} (4.7\%) and of the most robust alternative (8.8\%) are unchanged, because the replaced runs decide none of them; the margin of \pol{Full} over \pol{TIDE} on MoE grows from $+2.0$\% to $+15.6$\%.

\section{Controller Algorithm and Configuration}\label{app:controller}

\paragraph{Prediction model.} The controller predicts from the calibration file alone. With $\Theta_{\mathrm{plain}}(C)$, $T_{\mathrm{base}}(C)$, and $T_{\mathrm{spec}}(C)$ the measured plain throughput and the plain and speculative step times, interpolated in $\log_2 C$ between the calibrated loads, and $g=z\,\Ghat_\ell(a,C)+(1-z)$ the predicted gain of the current speculation state, it predicts the throughput $\Theta(a,z,C,d)=\Theta_{\mathrm{plain}}(C)\,g\,(1-\kappa(C)\,d)$ and the TPOT $\widehat T(a,z,C,d)=T_{\mathrm{base}}(C)/\big(g\,(1-\kappa(C)\,d)\big)$. For a domain without a frontier fit, $\Ghat$ falls back to the proportional form $a\,T_{\mathrm{base}}(C)/T_{\mathrm{spec}}(C)$. The learning curve $a_\ell(N)$ is interpolated in $\log(1+N)$ between the calibrated export points; its inverse gives the requests, and through the domain's mean tokens per request the tokens $n$, that separate $\hat a$ from a target acceptance. In the frozen runs the TPOT entering the rules is this prediction, not a measured latency. Algorithm~\ref{alg:controller} gives one control tick and \cref{tab:constants} the constants.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{One control tick (every second).}
\label{alg:controller}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Read $C$, the queue, the speculative-decoding counters, and the trainer's state; if more than 20 drafts were made in the last second, $\hat a\leftarrow0.7\,\hat a+0.3\,a_{\mathrm{1s}}$
\IF{the label $\ell$ changed}
\STATE reset the regime clock and $\hat a$, flush the trainer's buffer, restart the export schedule
\ENDIF
\IF{the trainer exported a new drafter}
\STATE hot-swap it; if speculation is off, enable it for 8\,s to measure the new drafter
\ENDIF
\IF{$\ell$ has no learning curve, or $\amax_\ell-\hat a\le0.02$, or the last swap raised $\hat a$ by less than 0.02}
\STATE $d\leftarrow0$
\ELSIF{$\Ghat_\ell(\amax_\ell,C)\le1$}
\STATE $d\leftarrow0$ \COMMENT{structural ceiling}
\ELSIF{predicted TPOT $\le\Tslo$ and the server is not saturated}
\STATE $d\leftarrow$ largest $d\in\mathcal D$ with $T_0/(1-\kappa(C)d)\le0.9\,\Tslo$
\ELSE
\STATE $d\leftarrow\arg\max_{d\in\mathcal D,\,d\le0.5}V(d)$ of \cref{eq:value} if the maximum is positive, else $0$
\ENDIF
\IF{the requests learned in this regime reached the next export point}
\STATE request an export; the next export point doubles the gap (100, 200, 400, \dots)
\ENDIF
\STATE compute $z^{\star}$ by \cref{eq:switch}; switch to $z^{\star}$ if it has differed from $z$ for 3 ticks, the last switch is at least 10\,s old, and no probe is active
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Fairness.} All policies except \pol{Production} run the same engine configuration, memory reserve, and export path, serve the same prompt sequence per repetition with the same trainer seed, and start from the factory drafter; they differ only in the information they may use (last column of \cref{tab:policies}) and in what they do with it. \pol{Production} has neither export path nor reserve, so its difference from \pol{Static} is the cost of the infrastructure (\cref{tab:mechanisms}). \pol{Informed} uses information no deployable policy has, the start of phase B and a stopping point read off the learning curves, and is a reference rather than a competitor; \pol{Always} and \pol{Threshold} use less information than \pol{Full}; \pol{Switch} and \pol{TIDE} use the same frontier as \pol{Full} but not the learning curve. The constants of every policy were fixed before the freeze (\cref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{The TIDE-style baseline.} \pol{TIDE} reproduces Algorithm~1 of TIDE \citep{park2026tide} in the co-located setting. Its speculation rule is that of \pol{Switch}. Collection is enabled when serving starts. Two moving averages of the measured acceptance length, with weights 0.3 and 0.02 on the newest second and initialised from the first 10 measured seconds, detect a distribution shift when the short one falls more than 0.1 below the long one; a shift re-enables collection. While collection is enabled the trainer runs at duty 0.5, the duty of the other fixed-duty baselines (TIDE itself trains on separate GPUs). After every 200 requests learned the trainer exports a drafter, which is swapped in and measured for 8\,s (with a speculation probe if speculation is off). It is kept if its acceptance is higher than that of the previous drafter just before the swap; otherwise the previous drafter is restored and collection is disabled until the next shift. TIDE leaves the averaging weights, the margin, and the round size unspecified, so these values are ours. TIDE evaluates candidates offline on held-out samples of the round; the frozen trainer has no evaluation path, so we evaluate live, and we declare an evaluation inconclusive, keeping the new drafter, when the traffic label changed within 8\,s before the swap or during the measurement, because the two drafters would then be measured on different traffic. The training rule never uses the label otherwise. \pol{TIDE} was added after the 72-run matrix, on the same frozen checkpoint: its controller and a wrapper that makes the frozen harness start it are new files (\cref{tab:manifest}); no frozen file was changed.

\subsection{Decision ablations: which test the gain comes from}\label{app:ablation}

The policies of \cref{tab:main} are partial ablations, but none isolates the payback rule, and \pol{Switch} removes training altogether. We therefore reran \pol{Full} with one of its tests removed, on the frozen checkpoint, prompts, and seeds (36 runs; \cref{tab:ablate} in \cref{sec:levers}): \emph{without the payback rule}, the trainer runs at duty 0.5 whenever the stop tests allow it under saturation; \emph{without the stop tests}, the structural-ceiling, at-ceiling, and convergence tests are skipped; \emph{without the switch}, speculation stays on and training is valued with speculation stuck on (\cref{app:controller}); \emph{without per-domain knowledge}, the controller ignores the traffic label and the learning curves: it predicts throughput with the per-target proportional frontier $\Ghat(a,C)=a\,T_{\mathrm{base}}(C)/T_{\mathrm{spec}}(C)$, assumes that the next round raises acceptance by 0.3, replaces that prior with the gain measured after every swap, and, after a round that did not pay, waits 120\,s before trying one more.

The gain comes from a different test in each scenario. On Chinese it comes entirely from the switch ($-43.8$\% without it, $-46.2$ to $-42.0$\% per repetition), while the other three variants stay within noise of \pol{Full}. On Qwen3-8B code it comes from training, and the payback rule is worth 2.5\% there: at a fixed duty the trainer runs until the ceiling test stops it (mean duty 0.16 against 0.067), and the interference costs more than the last drafters gain. On the MoE target every variant trains and stops at the ceiling, and no single test is decisive. The stop tests change nothing under saturation ($-0.2$\%, $+1.8$\%, $-0.9$\%): near the ceiling the payback rule already finds no value, and on Chinese it finds none even without the structural-ceiling test, so the three tests are shortcuts that matter only for the SLO-slack rule in the light phases. The variant without per-domain knowledge stays within noise of \pol{Full} in all three scenarios ($-0.6$\%, $-0.1$\%, $-0.9$\%): the switch needs only the measured acceptance and the per-target step times, and the measured swap gains replace the learning curve. Its one cost is timing: in one Chinese repetition ($-5.8$\%) a drafter trained in the slack reached its export point late in phase B, and the speculation probe after the swap ran at saturation on Chinese. Where the curves matter is traffic that a label does not describe, which \cref{app:mix} examines.

\section{Mixed-Traffic and Calibration Robustness}\label{app:mix}

The live workloads of \cref{tab:main} switch between single domains, and the controller is told which one it serves. Production traffic is often a mixture without a clean switch point, and the label can be stale. We therefore built two mixtures by interleaving, request by request, equal numbers of prompts from two pools, code with chat and Chinese with chat, and ran the policies of \cref{tab:main} on them with the same phases, seeds, and harness (42 runs). Each mixture was calibrated as a domain of its own by the profiling runs of \cref{app:calibration}: the code--chat drafter learns from an acceptance of 1.98 to a ceiling of 2.47, which at $C{=}256$ moves it from 0.88$\times$ to 1.07$\times$ plain decoding on the calibrated frontier (break-even at 2.29); the Chinese--chat drafter learns from 1.53 to 1.84 and stays below plain decoding (0.69$\times$ to 0.82$\times$). Two further variants of \pol{Full} test the label: with a \emph{stale label} the controller is told that phase B is still chat, so it uses chat's curve and frontier on the mixture's prompts; the \emph{label-free} variant of \cref{app:ablation} has neither label nor curve.

Neither mixture pays for speculation at saturation, so \cref{tab:mix} (in \cref{sec:robust}) ranks the policies by how much each loses on the way to that conclusion, and plain decoding is the best policy on both. On Chinese--chat the structural-ceiling test fires on the first tick of phase B: \pol{Full} turns speculation off (on for 3\% of the phase), does not train on the mixture (mean duty 0.01), and ends $-2.2$\% from \pol{Plain}, the cost of the speculation-off state (\cref{tab:mechanisms}), and within noise of \pol{Switch} ($+0.2$\%). The label-free variant reaches the same decision from the measured acceptance ($-0.2$\% from \pol{Full}). With the stale label the controller believes it serves chat, trains one round, swaps the drafter in, finds no gain, and stops; the wasted round costs 1.4\% in every repetition. \pol{TIDE} re-enables training on every drift it measures, swaps 5.3 drafters, and ends $-3.7$\% from \pol{Full}. Code--chat is a break-even mixture: the adapted drafter's ceiling is just above the break-even acceptance, so what it can return is within the error of the calibration, and every policy except \pol{Static} lands within 7.2\% of \pol{Plain}. \pol{Full} trains 2.0 rounds on average, stops when a swap no longer raises acceptance or the payback rule finds nothing left to earn, and keeps speculation on (99\% of the phase) because the calibrated frontier predicts a small gain at the acceptance it reaches; the rounds that get it there and the part of the phase served by the factory drafter cost more than the adapted drafter returns, and it ends $-2.7$\% behind \pol{Switch}, which turns speculation off at the factory drafter's acceptance, and $-4.7$\% behind \pol{Plain}. Here the curve is worth something: the label-free variant expects every round to raise acceptance by its prior and trains for longer (mean duty 0.14 against 0.04, 3.7 swaps) before the measured swap gains stop it, and pays 2.6\% more than \pol{Full}; the stale label costs 1.1\%, within noise.

Two caveats. The proportion of each mixture is fixed throughout phase B, because the benchmark client shuffles each prompt pool; a mixture whose proportion drifts within a regime is not covered. And on a break-even mixture the right decision is within the calibration error, so no policy that speculates can be expected to beat plain decoding there; what the controller contributes is to lose little (0.953 of the best policy) rather than to win.

\section{Cross-Engine Implementation and Results: SGLang}\label{app:sglang}

\paragraph{Port.} We reimplemented the three engine extensions of \cref{sec:system} on SGLang 0.5.10 \citep{zheng2024sglang}, as a runtime patch of its default EAGLE-3 path, which runs without SGLang's overlap scheduler. The live tap writes the records of \cref{sec:system} to the same ring buffer, so the trainer is unchanged; its rows are the prefill chunks, the accepted prefix of every verification, and the input token of every step with speculation off. A drafter swap copies the exported weights in place, skipping the embedding that the drafter shares with the target. With speculation off, a decode step runs the target on a second set of CUDA graphs, captured at start-up for plain decoding, with the bookkeeping of a verification that accepted no draft. As in vLLM, the drafter does not run while speculation is off; the first speculative step afterwards refreshes the drafter's inputs with one pass over every request's last committed position. A metrics endpoint with vLLM's names and an RPC endpoint let the harness, benchmark client, controller, trainer, prompt files, and seeds of the vLLM checkpoint run unchanged, with identical hashes.

\paragraph{Checks.} Before calibrating we checked the port on both targets. Greedy outputs with speculation on and off agreed on 8/8 prompts for Qwen3-8B and 8/8 for the MoE target. Toggling speculation every 0.5\,s under load (18 and 28 toggles) completed every request with exact token counts. Swapping drafters moved Qwen3-8B's acceptance on code from 2.03 (factory) to 2.59 (a code drafter from the vLLM runs) and 1.01 (its input layer zeroed), and back to 2.04, without changing the target's output. The exported hidden states match those of Hugging Face Transformers row by row, with cosine similarity at least 0.999 for Qwen3-8B and 0.993 for the MoE target.

\section{Frozen-Simulator Validation}\label{app:sim}

\paragraph{Model.} The simulator (\texttt{sched/sim.py}) advances in 1\,s steps on one GPU. Arrivals of the current domain set the operating concurrency $C$ by solving $\Theta(C)=$ demand, where $\Theta$ includes the frontier gain when speculation is on and the factor $1-\kappa(C)\,d$ when the trainer runs; demand beyond the capacity at $C{=}256$ accumulates in a queue. TPOT is the step time divided by the frontier gain and by $1-\kappa(C)\,d$. Served requests feed a training buffer; training at duty $d$ consumes tokens at rate $r\,d$ and moves acceptance along the domain's learning curve, with the measured fraction transferring to chat. Goodput counts output tokens served in seconds whose TPOT and queueing delay meet the SLO. The simulator and the calibration file were frozen before the validation (hashes in \cref{tab:manifest}).

\paragraph{Simple policies.} \pol{NoSpec} never speculates and \pol{SpecAlways} always does; neither trains. \pol{OSD}, \pol{Timed}, and \pol{AccDrop} keep speculation on and train at full duty while their trigger holds: fewer than 16 running requests, 30\,s every 5 minutes, and acceptance below 95\% of the domain's ceiling, respectively.

\paragraph{Workloads.} V1 replays BurstGPT \citep{wang2024burstgpt} chat logs on days 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, and 20 and API logs on days 15, 21, and 22 (development had used day 2 and day 10 only), each for 24\,h and scaled so that the 95th-percentile demand is 0.6, 0.8, or 1.0 times capacity, and the Azure 2023 chat and code traces \citep{patel2024splitwise} for one hour at scales 0.6--1.2. V2 draws 60 bursts: a chat, code, or function-calling phase, followed by a code, SQL, or function-calling burst at 1.0, 1.05, or 1.2 times capacity lasting 5, 10, 20, 60, 180, or 600\,s, and a recovery phase (\cref{tab:duration} reports it by burst length). V3 draws 40 regimes in which traffic switches to Chinese at 128--256 concurrent requests or 1.1 times capacity for 600--1{,}800\,s. V4 draws 20 scenarios with a calm phase of 120--900\,s, a moderate phase, and a 600\,s surge, each at seven surge levels from 0.9 to 1.2 times capacity.

\paragraph{Training volume and horizon.} The controller trains on 0.9--1.2M tokens per trace window, whereas \pol{OSD} trains on up to 304M. Replacing the horizon forecast with the true remaining length of the regime changes the outcome in 3 of 275 cases, so the forecast barely matters in these workloads.
